A gauntlet loop for empirical papers

Social Science Gauntlet

You have a finished manuscript and you want to know whether it clears the bar at a top-5 journal — before you send it, not four months later in a rejection letter. This is what the skill actually does, how it stops itself from cheating, and how it keeps you accountable for a paper it revised while you weren't watching.

It races your draft against a paper that actually got into the journal you're targeting, blind, judged by AI referees from two different companies — and keeps revising until it wins, or until the referees stop finding new problems.

The shape of the thing

Four setup steps that happen once, then a cycle that runs separately on each of seven tracks. The tracks are the objections that actually kill papers — not the sections a paper is organised into. Nobody rejects a manuscript for having a weak Section 3.

Why a published paper, and not a rubric

The obvious version of this is to hand your draft to a smart model and ask whether it's good enough for AJPS. That fails, and it's worth being precise about why: the model grades you against a standard it invented on the spot, and it wants to be helpful. Revise, ask again, and it says the draft is stronger now. The standard climbs with you.

The part that stops it cheating

Here is the danger, stated plainly. If you tell an AI to keep working until the referee stops complaining, and the referee complains that your result isn't robust, the cheapest way to make that complaint disappear is to find a version of the analysis where the result holds. That is p-hacking, automated and tireless. So the loop is allowed to run robustness checks, but only through a gate.

Worth saying out loud when you explain this: it is arguably more honest than how most of us work by hand. Nobody keeps a record of the checks that never made it into the appendix.

It never stops to ask you

That's deliberate. A run that halts every time it touches a claim is a run nobody finishes, and an author asked forty times will approve forty times without reading. But it has a real cost — decisions that are properly yours get made while you aren't watching. So the loop pays that cost three ways instead of pretending it isn't there.

First, a separate classifier triages every change it accepts into one that alters what the paper claims, one that's substantive but doesn't, and one that's routine. Not the builder — the builder has an interest in calling its own work minor. Then three standing rules make the loop's unsupervised choices safe by construction.

Never strengthen a claim

Where a change would alter what the paper asserts, it takes the weaker, more hedged, more narrowly scoped form. “Is consistent with” never becomes “shows”. This settles failed robustness checks too: a check that fails narrows the claim to where it holds. Bounding down needs no permission — bounding up would.

Never delete your words silently

Any sentence you wrote that the loop cuts or rewrites is preserved verbatim in the changelog, in full. You can restore any of it from the record without going back through the version history to find it.

Never add a citation you haven't read

Every reference the loop introduces is written into the draft with an inline [UNVERIFIED] marker and stays that way until you confirm you've read the work. You're accountable for every reference in the paper, and a log entry you might not open is not enough protection.

Markers are the one thing that blocks, and they block only at the end. Every claim-level change leaves a marker at the exact spot in the manuscript, and the paper isn't submittable while any marker remains. Clearing them is your obligatory work, and it's proportional to how much the loop actually changed.

The run finishes with a handoff brief rather than a verdict: the claims diff first — the paper's assertions as they now stand, against the ones you wrote down before round one — then the claim-level changes ordered by how far they moved the paper, the open markers, every robustness check that failed and how it narrowed things, and the objections that were still live when each track ran dry. Those last ones are what a real referee will raise. Ordered by risk, not by date.

When it stops

Three different finish lines, because pretending there's one would be a lie.

TrackHow it ends
Writing, tables, figures It ends when your version beats the published paper, four comparisons out of four. This is genuinely winnable — you can out-present a paper that's already in print.
Contribution It will never beat a published paper's contribution; that took someone three years. So it ends when two consecutive rounds pass without a new objection. Repeated objections don't reset the count.
Identification, measurement Same running-dry rule, with a floor: an objection your design genuinely cannot answer goes into the limitations section, not into another round. Loop on what's fixable. Disclose what isn't.
Every track Nothing ends while the replication code fails to run, a number in a table doesn't match what the code produced, or a check that was declared in the ledger is missing from the manuscript.
And then you “Every track has hit its exit” means there is nothing left for the loop to do. It does not mean the paper is ready. Submission is an authorial act — you clear the handoff brief and the markers. The loop reaches done. Only you reach ready.

Say the limits out loud

These three are the difference between a tool and a toy, so lead with them when someone is sceptical.

The comparison paper may already be memorised

If the AI recognises the published paper, it isn't comparing two manuscripts — it's remembering which one is famous. Use papers published after the models' training cutoff, and probe each referee once per run to check it doesn't recognise either one.

Two referees from one company are one referee

Models from the same family share training data and taste, so their agreement carries far less information than it looks like. That's why the panel is deliberately cross-vendor rather than two of the same model.

Running dry is not the same as passing

Two rounds without a new objection means the machine is out of ideas. It does not mean you have a top-5 paper. Read the accumulated objections yourself before you decide anything.

And what it isn't. It doesn't predict whether you'll get in. It doesn't replace a human referee. It cannot make a boring finding interesting. What it does is burn through the obvious objections before a real referee reaches them, with machinery that makes it hard to fool yourself along the way.

The gauntlet loop technique is Matt Shumer's, from the original prompt he wrote for Claude of Duty. This adapts gauntlet-loop by Jay E at RoboNuggets, which packaged that technique as a reusable skill.
